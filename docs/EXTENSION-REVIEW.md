# PT25003 consultation — design rationale and extension review

**For:** the project team, and anyone asked to review the consultation instrument.
**Instrument:** https://consultation.agaims.com.au
**Status:** pilot round open, September 2026.

This sets out why the consultation is built the way it is, checks the design against the
frameworks Australian agricultural extension uses, and lists what a reviewer is asked to look at.
It is a desk review by the project, not an independent one — the last section says where outside
review would add most.

---

## 1. What the instrument is for

The Potato Mechanisation Project needs direction from across the value chain on where
mechanisation, automation and better digital tools would make the most practical difference —
and it needs to find the people willing to help: trial hosts, demonstration sites, case studies,
and members for the project reference group.

The conventional route is a general meeting plus a ring-around. Both are expensive in time and
travel, both favour whoever can attend or picks up the phone, and a ring-around produces a hundred
conversations that cannot be compared with each other because each was asked slightly
differently.

The instrument is a first pass that is cheap for the project, wide geographically, and
**comparable across every method used to collect it** — online, phone, one-to-one, and later
workshops and grower-group discussions. It does not replace those other methods. It makes them
count towards the same picture.

## 2. Design decisions

| Decision | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| **Role-based branches.** Respondents pick a perspective (grower, farm manager, contractor, processor or packhouse, machinery supplier, technology provider, adviser, industry body) and only see questions about work they do. | The value chain is too varied for one list. A generic survey asks everyone something slightly wrong, and people skip it or answer vaguely. |
| **Tick-boxes where the answer space is known; open text where it is not.** | Structured answers are faster to give and can be counted. Five open questions remain, deliberately, where the value is what the project did not think to ask. |
| **About ten minutes, on a phone, anonymous by default.** | Respect for respondents' time is the strongest single driver of completion. No account, no download, no password. |
| **Questions worded as a person would ask them aloud.** | The same wording is used on the phone, so a phone answer and a form answer are the same answer. |
| **A phone route offered on the front page, with a generated script.** | An online-only invitation quietly biases findings towards people comfortable with web forms. |
| **Contact details held separately from answers, with no link between them.** | Anonymity is a mechanism, not a promise: nobody, including the project team, can attach a name to a set of answers. |
| **Scale asked as a tonnage band.** | Tonnage is the one measure a grower, packer and processor share. Without scale, a finding cannot be attributed to small operations or to the whole industry. |
| **Expressions of interest phrased by role.** | "Helping with a trial" means a paddock to a grower, a machine to a dealer, and a line to a packhouse. A generic tick produces a list nobody can act on. |
| **Partial answers not kept; only how far a session got.** | People are told they may stop at any time. Keeping the views of somebody who chose not to submit would contradict that. |
| **Rounds.** Question sets are versioned; each response carries its round. | The pilot never contaminates the real results, and repeating core questions in a later round gives a before-and-after measure. |

## 3. Check against extension frameworks

### ADOPT — Adoption and Diffusion Outcome Prediction Tool (CSIRO; Kuehne et al., 2017)

Widely used in Australian agricultural RD&E, including by GRDC and Hort Innovation. It predicts
adoption from characteristics of the practice and of the people who would adopt it.

| ADOPT factor | Where the instrument addresses it |
| --- | --- |
| Relative advantage (profit, time, risk) | Business impact of the top constraint; the measures people use to judge an investment |
| Trialability | *Added after this review:* how much it matters to try something on part of the operation first |
| Observability, and ease of learning | What evidence would convince them — local demonstrations, case studies, peer experience |
| Scale and relevance to the business | Tonnage band; role-specific branches |
| Advisory support and networks | *Added after this review:* whose opinion actually counts |
| Upfront cost, complexity, compatibility | Barriers to adoption; compatibility with existing equipment and data systems |

### Diffusion of innovations (Rogers)

People adopt what offers a clear advantage, fits what they already do, is simple enough, can be
tried on a small scale, and can be seen working. Each of those five appears in the evidence,
barriers and trial questions. The trusted-sources question maps the communication channels
through which diffusion happens.

### Farmer-first and participatory extension (Chambers and others)

Growers set part of the agenda rather than only ranking the project's. The open questions ask
what the project should do, what it should not spend money on, and what is being promoted that
will not work here — the last because nobody ticks a box to disagree with an industry
consensus.

### Adult and experiential learning (Knowles; Kolb)

Relevance to one's own work, and learning by seeing and doing, underpin the role branches and the
preference questions: field demonstrations, peer groups and case studies with numbers in them.

### Bennett's hierarchy (Bennett and Rockwell, Targeting Outcomes of Programs)

The standard model for evaluating extension, running from inputs and participation through
reactions, knowledge and attitude change, to practice change and end results. This consultation
sits at the start: it is the baseline and the needs analysis. Because the core questions are
stable and versioned by round, repeating them later gives a measure of change against the same
baseline — this should be planned deliberately, not discovered afterwards.

### Survey practice (Dillman, Tailored Design Method)

Low burden, a clear reason to respond, trust in how the data will be used, and a promise to
report back. The instrument is short, optional throughout, explains its purpose and privacy in
plain words, and offers respondents the summary of findings.

### Research ethics (NHMRC National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research)

Voluntary participation, informed consent before any question, anonymity by default, minimal
data, separation of identity from responses, and a named privacy contact. Whether formal ethics
review is required depends on how findings will be published; the project should confirm this
with Hort Innovation.

## 4. Gaps found, and what was done

| Gap | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Trialability was implied but never asked | Added: *"Before you committed to something new, how much would it matter to try it on part of the operation first?"* |
| Nothing identified who respondents trust | Added: *"When you are weighing up new gear or a new way of doing things, whose opinion actually counts?"* |
| Evaluation not designed in | **Done:** a baseline-and-review design with a locked set of tracked questions — see section 5 |
| Some technical terms remain ("interoperability") | Glossed on screen; to be tested with growers reading cold |
| Group discussions could be miscounted as individual responses | Planned: group sessions recorded as group records with counts, never as individual rows |

## 5. Evaluation plan: baseline and review

The consultation is the project's baseline, and it is repeated to measure change — the
before-and-after that Bennett's hierarchy and Hort Innovation's monitoring and evaluation plans ask
for.

### Schedule

| Round | When | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pilot | Now | The team tests the instrument. Never compared with anything. |
| **Baseline** | At launch | The starting point every later round is measured against. |
| **Mid-project review** | Roughly half-way | Early read on change, and a chance to adjust course. |
| **End-of-project review** | Before final reporting | The measure of change the final report rests on. |

Three measured rounds rather than annual ones, because asking the same people too often wears out
the goodwill the consultation depends on.

### Tracked questions

Asked word for word in every round, and locked against editing in the admin area and in the data
layer. Changing a tracked question — even adding an option, which draws answers away from the
others — would break the comparison.

| Measures | Tracked questions | Bennett level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the pressure is | Constraints; top three ranked | Situation |
| Priorities | Ratings across the twelve areas | Situation |
| **What is in use** | What growers and packhouses have adopted, trialled or considered | **Practice change** |
| What holds people back | Barriers to adoption | Situation / practice |
| What it takes to convince them | Evidence needed; importance of trying small first | Attitudes and aspirations |
| Who they listen to | Whose opinion counts | Knowledge pathways |
| Scale | Tonnage band | For segmenting every other measure |
| **What they saw** | *Review rounds only:* what project outputs they have seen or used | **Reactions** |
| **What changed** | *Review rounds only:* whether it changed what they do or plan to do | **Practice change** |

Role and region are asked in every round as well, since every comparison needs them.

Everything else may change between rounds, so topical questions can be added without disturbing
the comparison.

### What the comparison can and cannot claim

Responses are anonymous, and the project decided not to offer a code that would link one person's
answers across rounds. Each round is therefore a separate picture of the industry, and the
comparison is between pictures — a **repeated cross-section**, not a panel.

That supports statements such as *"the share of growers using optical grading rose from 18% to
31% between baseline and the end of the project."* It supports weaker claims about *why*, because
different people answer each round. Two safeguards:

- **Composition is reported beside every comparison**, by role, so a change in who answered is not
  read as a change of mind.
- **Figures from fewer than ten people are flagged** as thin.

Attribution to the project rests on the review-round questions — what people saw from the project,
and whether it changed what they did — together with the qualitative follow-up asked alongside
them.

## 6. The "is it wanky" test

The grower-facing test is plain words, obvious usefulness, and no sense of being studied. The
instrument avoids consultancy language, never uses "stakeholder engagement", gives a phone number
to a named person, and explains in one line why each piece of personal information is asked for.

The honest test is not this document. It is two or three growers reading it cold, with somebody
watching where they hesitate.

## 7. What a reviewer is asked to look at

1. Do the role branches cover the value chain, and is anybody missing?
2. Are the tick-box options the right ones, in growers' own words?
3. Are the five open questions the right ones to leave open?
4. Is anything likely to read as jargon, or as the project talking to itself?
5. Is the tracked set right for measuring change, and is anything missing from it?
6. Does the phone and group-discussion approach keep answers genuinely comparable?
7. Does anything here raise a research ethics question for publication?

Useful reviewers: an extension practitioner through APEN (the Australasia-Pacific Extension
Network), Hort Innovation's evaluation contact, and a small number of growers from different
regions.
